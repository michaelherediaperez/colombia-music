Short intro: my city, Manizales — mountains, coffee, the Nevado del Ruiz volcano. Point at the line on the small world map: we are on opposite sides of the Pacific. (~30 s)

First things first

Where is Colombia?

In the north-west corner of South America — the only country there with coasts on both the Pacific and the Caribbean.

Show the world first: many classmates have never placed Colombia on a map. Click once for the two-oceans fact. (~20 s)

Geography

Three Andes,
two oceans

The Andes split into three ranges — West, Central and East — with peaks over 5,000 m.

Two great rivers, the Magdalena and the Cauca, run north between them to the Caribbean.

Around them: two coasts, endless plains and the Amazon rainforest. Each place grew isolated — and grew its own culture.

Mountain ranges and rivers drawn approximately

Click 1: the three cordilleras. Click 2: the two rivers, with their names. Click 3: lowlands. Key idea: mountains separated people for centuries, so each region developed its own music. (~45 s)

Six natural regions

One country,
six worlds

Name each region quickly; point to where they are on the map. Climate depends on altitude, not seasons: hot coasts, cool mountains, snowy peaks. (~40 s)

Postcards

Every climate
in one country

Each click pops a place out of the map, one per region. Tatacoa: a desert in the middle of the Andes. Nevado del Ruiz: the glacier volcano above my city. Las Lajas: a church built across a canyon near Ipiales, Nariño. San Andrés: the “sea of seven colours”. (~60 s)

First peoples

Each valley,
its own people

Before 1500, each region had its own cultures — Muisca on the high plateau, Tayrona in the Sierra Nevada, Quimbaya in my coffee region. Many peoples still live in their territories today.

Point at the names on the map. Red dots are peoples living today; gold diamonds are ancient cultures (e.g. Quimbaya gold at the Museo del Oro, San Agustín statues). (~40 s)

Three roots

Then three worlds met

Each region mixed them in different amounts — so each region sounds different.

The key idea: the same three ingredients, mixed in different proportions depending on geography and history. Coasts: more African. Mountains: more Spanish and Indigenous. (~40 s)

Our national instrument

El tiple

Born in the Andes from the Spanish guitar family. Small body, bright voice — the heart of the bambuco and the pasillo.

12

steel strings in
4 groups of 3

The tiple: 12 strings in four groups of three, so every note sounds like a little choir. Played with the bandola and guitar in the classic Andean trio. Play the clip. (~35 s)

Five more voices

Listen to the regions

Source: “The sound of music: traditional Colombian instruments”, colombia.co · colombia.co/en/…/sound-music-traditional-colombian-instruments

One instrument per region: accordion and guacharaca (Caribbean, vallenato), marimba and cununo (Pacific), harp (Llanos). Play two or three of the sounds, not all — keep it moving. (~45 s)

The musical map

12 musical axes

Colombia’s National Library groups our folk music into twelve ejes musicales — and they follow the geography.

Source: “Ejes musicales de Colombia”, Wikipedia (es) · borders approximate

The musical map almost copies the natural regions map. Then: “Let’s travel through them.” (~30 s)

To sum up

Mountains separated us.
Music tells the story.

Three roots, six regions, twelve musical axes — and hundreds of rhythms. That is why there is no single “Colombian music”.
